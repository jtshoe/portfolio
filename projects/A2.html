Subtheme: Exploring Corporate Owners & Housing Market Dynamics

Overall Analysis Questions

  1. Who is doing most of the property flipping in Boston, and how has the composition of flippers changed over time?
    Motivation: Prior housing research often highlights the growing role of institutional and corporate investors in housing markets. In the NYTimes article on evictions, large-scale property ownership and professionalized management structures are shown to shape housing instability and displacement pressures. While I cannot directly study evictions with my datasets, I can examine whether flipping activity is increasingly dominated by corporate, institutional, and LLC-based investors rather than individual or small-scale buyers, which serves as a structural indicator of market financialization.
  2. What do price changes on flipped properties look like, and which types of investors capture the largest gains?
    Motivation: The article emphasizes how housing increasingly functions as a financial asset rather than a social good, with displacement often emerging from profit-driven market dynamics. From my own experience living in high-cost housing markets, I have seen rapid price escalation justified through "renovations" and "value-add" improvements. This question examines how large those gains actually are, how they are distributed, and whether profit concentration differs across investor types.
  3. How do financing strategies (cash vs mortgage purchases) differ across investor types and property categories?
    Motivation: Cash purchasing power is often associated with investor advantage, market competition, and displacement pressures. In both public discourse and personal experience, cash buyers are perceived as outcompeting individual homebuyers. This question explores whether corporate and institutional investors structurally rely more on cash transactions and how that behavior differs across property types and time.
  4. How concentrated is property ownership and transaction activity among investors?
    Motivation: The NYTimes article illustrates how housing systems become extractive when power and control concentrate in a small number of actors. This question investigates whether market influence is diffuse or concentrated by examining how many properties buyers and sellers actually own and trade, helping reveal structural concentration in Boston’s housing market.

Discoveries & Insights

Summary

Rather than treating each visualization as a standalone finding, this project uses each result to generate deeper lines of inquiry: How does investor dominance differ across neighborhoods and property types? Are profit gains driven by scale, financing strategies, or timing advantages? Do corporate actors behave uniformly, or do distinct investor classes exhibit different market logics? How do ownership concentration and transaction volume interact to produce market power? These follow-up questions move the analysis beyond descriptive patterns toward systemic interpretation, enabling richer insight into how financialization, capital access, and institutional participation reshape housing markets over time.

This exploratory analysis reveals a housing market increasingly shaped by corporate, institutional, and large-scale investors rather than individual owner-occupants. Corporate ownership rates rise consistently across neighborhoods, while owner-occupancy remains largely static. Flipping activity shows growing institutional dominance, with profit gains increasingly concentrated among large investors. Financing strategies further reinforce structural power imbalances, as cash transactions become more common among professional buyers. Finally, ownership and transaction concentration patterns indicate that market influence is held by a relatively small number of actors. Together, these patterns suggest a financialized housing system where property functions primarily as an investment vehicle, with implications for affordability, stability, and long-term community displacement.
